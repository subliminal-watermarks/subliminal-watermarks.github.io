<div class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**TL;DR**

Are the current watermarking schemes robust against distillation attacks? We first introduce two simple techniques that beat all anti-distillation watermark detectors we tested: **grandchild** and **entropy laundering**. Inspired by subliminal learning, we suspect that the watermark signal may generalize better in **parameter space**, and introduce **Subliminal Watermark (SubWM)**, a new detection scheme that can identify distillation from a few sampled rollouts of a suspect model and remains effective after the laundering schemes. It measures "how much does the likelihood of this sequence increase if we shift our probe model's weights toward a watermarked one?" 

On science questions, SubWM achieves about **99%** true positive rate (TPR) and **3%** false positive rate (FPR) per **single** query to the suspect. This also means that a simple majority vote over, say, 10 sampled rollouts is projected to detect distillation almost perfectly. On **long-horizon** coding tasks, the detection is more difficult, likely because code tokens have lower entropy, but SubWM still achieves above **65%** TPR at **5%** FPR. A majority vote over only 50 sampled rollouts again is projected to achieve almost perfect detection. To avoid data leakage, both the evaluation and threshold tuning are done on **held-out** prompts that neither SubWM nor the suspect has ever seen during training.

## 1. What are LLM watermarks? {#watermark}

When a frontier model is served through an API, anyone can collect the responses and train their own model on them. This is known as _distillation_. When done without permission, it raises legal, safety, and provenance concerns ([Anthropic, 2026](#ref-anthropic2026)). The model provider needs a way to know:
- Is this response generated by my model?
- Is this response generated by a model that distills from mine?
- Does their model distill from mine?

The standard tool is _watermarking_ ([Aaronson, 2022](#ref-aaronson2022)): embed statistical patterns into the LLM's responses while disturbing the logit distribution as little as possible. Sometimes the second question is called _antidistillation fingerprinting_ ([Xu et al., 2026](#ref-xu2026)), but we will simply use the term _watermark_. Of course, if we can answer the second question, then we also solve the third one if we can query the model, e.g., majority vote on the queried responses from the suspect. 

Watermarking is also becoming a legal requirement. From 2026, the EU AI Act requires providers of generative AI systems, text generators included, to mark their outputs in a machine-readable format that is detectable as AI-generated ([European Union, 2024](#ref-euaiact2024), [European Commission, 2026](#ref-eccop2026)).

One of the earliest examples of watermarking is Aaronson's ([2022](#ref-aaronson2022)) Gumbel-Max watermark. The idea is simple: since there is intrinsic randomness in LLM sampling at non-zero temperature anyway, we could swap in pseudorandomness that can be deterministically verified later.

More concretely, we prepare an $$n$$-gram hash table: at each step, the previous $$n$$ tokens and a secret key $$k$$ are hashed into a seed, and the seed generates one pseudorandom number $$u_v \in (0,1)$$ for every token $$v$$ in the vocabulary. Given the model's next-token probabilities $$p_v$$, we return $$\arg\max_v u_v^{1/p_v}$$, the standard Gumbel-Max trick. Because the seed depends only on the preceding $$n$$ tokens and the key, one can recompute every $$u_v$$ afterwards with the key. 

The widget below helps visualize the effect of key selection on the model's output.

To anyone without the key, $$u$$ looks uniformly random, so each token is still chosen with probability $$p_v$$.


The detection in the original work computes the _textual_ correlation between sampled tokens $$y_t$$ and the pseudorandom numbers $$u_{y_t}$$ by $$ S(y) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} -\ln\big(1 - u^{(t)}_{y_t}\big).$$ Again, the number $$u_{y_t}$$ can be recomputed from the key $$k$$ and the preceding $$n$$ tokens. 

Follow-ups, such as SynthID ([Dathathri et al., 2024](#ref-dathathri2024)) and TextSeal ([Sander et al., 2026](#ref-sander2026)), all work similarly but improve the quality-detectability tradeoff. Green/Red-list ([Kirchenbauer et al., 2023](#ref-kirchenbauer2023)) and ADFP ([Xu et al., 2026](#ref-xu2026)) work slightly differently, but we won't elaborate further. This is because none of these detection methods survives the simple attacks introduced in the next section, and once we switch to our new detection method, Subliminal Watermark (SubWM), even the vanilla Gumbel-Max watermark becomes detectable after the attacks.

## 2. Two simple attacks beat previous detection methods {#laundering}

Are the current watermarking schemes robust against smart distillers? Unfortunately, the answer is _no_. We introduce two simple attacks.

**Grandchild laundering.** Train a *child* on the watermarked teacher traces as usual, but don't deploy it. Instead, sample the child on the same prompts, keep the correct answers, and train a fresh *grandchild* from the original checkpoint on those. The grandchild never sees a watermarked token.

## 3. SubWM: a novel, robust detection method in parameter space {#robust-detection}


### 3.1 The detector

Let $$\theta_0$$ be the teacher's parameters. On its own prompts, the provider samples clean traces $$D_c$$ and watermarked traces $$D'_w$$, then fine-tunes two copies of the teacher with identical settings:

$$
\theta_c = \mathrm{SFT}(\theta_0, D_c), \qquad \theta_w = \mathrm{SFT}(\theta_0, D'_w).
$$

The two datasets differ only in the watermark, so $$\Delta\theta = \theta_w - \theta_c$$ is a watermark direction in weight space. For a suspect prompt-response pair $$(x, y)$$, the *likelihood score* is

$$
\begin{equation}
s(x, y) \;=\; \frac{1}{|y|} \Big\langle \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(y \mid x)\big|_{\theta_c},\; \Delta\theta \Big\rangle \;=\; \frac{1}{|y|}\, \frac{d}{d\epsilon} \log p_{\theta_c + \epsilon \Delta\theta}(y \mid x) \Big|_{\epsilon = 0} . \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
$$

In words: 

*How much more likely is the response if we shift the clean teacher toward the watermarked one in parameter space?*
{: .key-idea}

Crucially, the score can be computed very efficiently via **autodiff**. We just need one forward pass through the slightly perturbed weights $$\theta_c + \epsilon\Delta\theta$$ and then a backward pass to the scalar $$\epsilon$$. The gradients are _never materialized_. This is because our score is _not_ the cosine similarity between two gradients or two parameter-space directions, but the canonical pairing between one of each (or between the tangent space and the cotangent space if you wish). This is why we don't need to, e.g., normalize the gradients and $$\Delta\theta$$ in principle, or approximate the Hessian of the loss landscape.

In fact, even without backprop, the finite-difference approximation already works well:

$$
\begin{equation}
s(x, y) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{|y|} \log \frac{p_{\theta_w}(y \mid x)}{p_{\theta_c}(y \mid x)} , \label{eq:llr}
\end{equation}
$$

which is just the **mean log-likelihood ratio** (LLR) between the teacher checkpoints. Despite its strong performance in practice, this simple score is less interpretable in terms of linear parameter-space directions.


### 3.2 SubWM has great TPR and FPR on single responses, and even out of distribution

All students in this post run in **thinking mode**, so SubWM scores the model's full chain of thought (many existing anti-distillation methods work poorly on long contexts). In particular, for agentic coding, each action is generated with up to about 29k tokens of the trajectory in context.

This detector, trained on science questions, works for other domains too, including math (GSM8K) and coding (LiveCodeBench). We ask the same science-distilled students new questions from these domains and score their answers with the same detector, recalibrating only the threshold on clean teacher answers from the new domain. On math, the LLR score flags **99.3%** of watermarked answers at a **5.9%** FPR; on coding questions, **89.4%** at **5.8%** (see the [out-of-distribution table](#ood-results)).

For agentic coding tasks, the task is a lot harder, likely because code tokens usually have lower entropy and thereby less room to embed statistical patterns. So, we trained a separate detector on agentic coding (a Qwen3-4B student distilled from SWE-smith trajectories). SubWM flags **67%** of watermarked actions at a **5.5%** FPR. Not as good as before, but a few dozen queries still settle the model-level detection (as explained later in [§3.5](#model-detection)).

### 3.3 SubWM can work with two different keys at the same time 

We can detect not only distillation, but also the _source_ of distillation! With detectors for both key 1 and key 2, the contrast $$s_1 - s_2$$ of their scores **separates the key-1 and key-2 students perfectly** (AUROC 1.000 on 800 held-out prompts). All 800 key-1 responses have positive contrast, and all 800 key-2 responses have negative contrast.

### 3.4 SubWM is robust to laundering

We evaluate the entropy-laundered and grandchild students with the original detector, using the LLR score. On science questions, TPRs stay above 99% at below 3% FPR, essentially **the same as without laundering**. In agentic coding, grandchild laundering does not lower detection either (67% → 69% TPR at 4–6% FPR).

### 3.5 SubWM could perfectly detect model distillation by majority vote {#model-detection}

We have presented our results on _rollout_ detection. What about _model_ detection? A simple strategy is to query $$N$$ independent responses from the suspect and do a strict **majority vote**. For $$N=10$$, at least six responses must pass; a five–five tie counts as negative.

If the rollouts are independent, the TPR and FPR of the vote follow from a simple binomial calculation (see the [appendix](#majority-vote)). With only $$N=10$$ queries, this would give **perfect** detection on science questions: with the LLR score, the projected TPR rounds to 100% in every science setting, with or without laundering, while the projected FPR stays below $$1.4 \times 10^{-7}$$ (see the [result tables](#result-tables)).

On coding tasks, we could increase the number of queries to $$N=50$$ (which is still very small) and expect near-perfect detection: for the agentic-coding lineage, $$N=50$$ gives a projected TPR of 99.1% without laundering and 99.7% after grandchild laundering, at a projected FPR below $$10^{-19}$$.

We haven't tested SubWM on multiple clean models and measured FPR directly, which is an important next step for us. In the meantime, we would like to emphasize that
1. Our detector works on OOD tasks.
2. Our detector is completely student-agnostic; from the training traces to threshold calibration, everything is agnostic to the distiller. 

So, we think it is reasonable that the independence assumption for the above calculation holds up.

### 3.6 Why does SubWM work though?

As the name suggests, SubWM is inspired by subliminal learning: a model trained on another model's outputs could learn behaviors beyond the semantics and format of the outputs, which could be explained by linear directions in the parameter space ([Cloud et al., 2025](#ref-cloud2025)). At the moment, we do not have a clean connection yet, but we have the following interesting observation.  

"If SubWM is just a likelihood test, do we really need to finetune the teacher? Can't we just use the Gumbel-Max distribution to compute the watermarked likelihood directly?" It is not obvious how to do this because Gumbel-Max is a deterministic algorithm, so there is no distribution. Nonetheless, we tried a few versions of this, such as that of [Ma (2026)](#ref-ma2026). Although they all worked well on the teacher-generated texts, none survives distillation.

This seems to suggest two things. First, detectors based on the watermark's sampling rules are fragile to distillation. The key to SubWM's success might be to look for watermark signals in **the parameter space**. This does not have to be the parameter space of the student. In our case, we use the teacher's, but we expect any reasonably sized model would work. Second, whatever the teacher and the student both learn, it is **not** decoding the Gumbel-Max pseudorandomness. This makes sense, because we shouldn't be able to invert hash functions by simply running SFT on some tokens! 

It is interesting to investigate what exactly the models learn from watermarked traces, and why it is universal across models of different sizes (4B and 9B in our case). We expect some techniques from the subliminal learning literature would be useful, but we leave that to future work.

**Majority vote, science (§3.5).**
{: #majority-vote}

If the $$N$$ rollouts are independent and each is flagged with the single-response rates reported above, TPR $$t$$ for a watermarked student and FPR $$q$$ for a clean one, then the strict-majority vote has

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{FPR}_N &= \Pr\!\left[\operatorname{Binomial}(N,q) \ge \lfloor N/2\rfloor+1\right],\\
\mathrm{TPR}_N &= \Pr\!\left[\operatorname{Binomial}(N,t) \ge \lfloor N/2\rfloor+1\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Training and sampling

- **Teacher and students.** The science experiments use the same science-SFT Qwen3.5-9B adapter. Their students start from Qwen3.5-4B and use LoRA rank 32. Each first-generation clean or keyed student is SFT-trained on 9,000 teacher traces; grandchildren train on child-generated traces.
- **Science detector rollouts.** Thinking mode; at most 1,024 new tokens per response. Sampling uses temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, and presence penalty 1.5.
- **Science detector scoring.** The science-fitted $$D'_w$$ detectors are scored in FP32. The main science, entropy, grandchild, and clean-teacher calibration results retokenize returned response text without adding a synthetic EOS; explicit terminal special-token strings remain part of that text. Scoring keeps the full prompt and the first response tokens up to a 1,024-token total limit; only response tokens enter the per-token score.
- **Watermarks.** The science detector experiments use vanilla Gumbel-Max (TextSeal with $$\alpha = 0$$), a 3-token hash context, and keys $$k_1, k_2$$. The baseline laundering comparisons in §2 use TextSeal with $$\alpha = 0.1$$, SynthID, and ADFP. ADFP uses $$\lambda = 256$$ in the grandchild table and $$\lambda = 64$$ in the entropy table.

#### Science prompt partitions and detector labels

- $$D'_w$$ is the fresh detector-training corpus; $$D_w$$ denotes the student's training corpus, not a second set of detector results. This post excludes detector results fitted on $$D_w$$.
- **Decontamination.** The recorded near-duplicate audit found no matches between fresh detector-training, student-training, and detector-evaluation prompts. Our additional exact-prompt check found zero overlap between the 9,000 fresh detector-training prompts and either of the two 1,000-prompt science evaluation pools. The two evaluation pools also have no exact-prompt overlap.
- **Detector evaluation.** The main two-key study uses one 1,000-prompt science pool; entropy and grandchild laundering share another. We retain their original 400 development and 400 confirmation prompts and combine them into 800 evaluation prompts per study. The remaining 200 prompts were used in earlier student-calibrated analyses; they do not set the clean-teacher cutoffs reported here.
- **Clean-teacher calibration.** We generate 1,000 unwatermarked responses from the source teacher $$\theta_0$$ with the same thinking-mode sampling settings as the student evaluations. These prompts are disjoint from student training, detector training, all earlier science evaluation pools, and the additional student-calibration prompt pool. Both keys score this same teacher corpus. The clean source teacher $$\theta_0$$ is distinct from the fitted clean detector reference $$\theta_c$$.
- **Threshold.** For each key and score, sort the 1,000 clean source-teacher scores and use the 951st-smallest as the cutoff. Flag a response only if its score is **strictly greater** than that cutoff. Freeze the teacher cutoffs before computing the student-transfer results; clean-student responses do not fit these thresholds. The same key-1 cutoffs apply unchanged to the main, entropy, and grandchild students. The nominal 5% teacher-calibration target does not guarantee a 5% FPR for a different clean model.
- **Key contrast.** The zero line for $$s_1 - s_2$$ compares the two key scores. It is not the threshold for deciding whether a response is watermarked.
- **Uncertainty and scope.** Teacher cutoffs are frozen before the transfer analysis, but the student evaluation responses had been inspected historically, so these are exploratory transfer results with one fitted student per condition. Science response-rate intervals use exact Clopper–Pearson bounds; AUROC intervals use paired-prompt bootstrap resamples. A bootstrap interval of [1, 1] for perfect ranking is not a guarantee of zero population error. Response resampling does not replicate model training, and the majority-vote numbers in §3.5 are conditional independent-query projections.

#### Coding detection

We also test grandchild laundering in a separate **Qwen3-4B** coding lineage. The source teacher is fine-tuned on successful trajectories from [Kimi-2.5 SWESmith](https://huggingface.co/datasets/open-athena/Kimi-2.5-swesmith-sandboxes-with_tests-oracle_verified_120s-maxeps-32k). As before, clean and keyed children distill the teacher's actions; grandchildren distill actions regenerated by the corresponding child with watermarking disabled. Each descendant starts from the original base checkpoint. The learned detector uses clean and keyed reference models fitted on separate coding tasks.

The agentic-coding experiment uses its own SWESmith-SFT Qwen3-4B source teacher and separately fitted coding references. Keyed coding traces use TextSeal with $$\alpha = 0$$ and a 3-token hash context. Generation allows up to 28,672 prompt tokens and 4,096 response tokens within a 32,768-token context. Coding LLR scores the original generated response token IDs, including a terminal EOS when emitted, conditioned on the full prompt.

Calibration generates unwatermarked actions from that source teacher at temperature 1, top-k 20, top-p 0.95, with a 4,096-token response budget; watermark injection is disabled and outputs are not filtered by success. Each detector uses the 951st-smallest score from the 1,000-action clean-teacher pool, with a strict greater-than decision. This gives a shared child/grandchild cutoff of **0.014877768768661323** for per-token LLR and **1.5491128392910267** for the conventional TextSeal score $$-\log_{10}p$$. Confirmation models and scores are held fixed while replacing the earlier clean-student calibration. Coding and science use their own fitted references and thresholds.

#### Baseline laundering evaluations (§2)

- **Grandchild table.** For SynthID and TextSeal, the $$p$$-values use 3,000 traces the child trained on. The clean control gives SynthID 0.35 and TextSeal 0.82.
- **ADFP.** The displayed grandchild $$p$$-values use the teacher's training traces. At teacher-trace prefixes, the evaluator samples the student's next token and measures its keyed green-list membership, keeping the first occurrence of each evaluated context bigram. The reported $$p$$-value is the one-sided Hoeffding bound: $$p = \exp[-2N(\bar g - \gamma)^2]$$ when $$\bar g > \gamma$$, and $$p = 1$$ otherwise, with green-list fraction $$\gamma = 0.5$$. Here $$N$$ is the number of retained measurements and $$\bar g$$ their mean. It is a corpus-level statistic, not a per-response FPR. The clean training $$p$$-value was not reported for this ADFP grandchild comparison.
- **Entropy table.** Detection columns report the usual $$p$$-value, evaluated on training traces. Accuracy uses 1,000 held-out science MCQs with thinking-mode sampling (temperature 1, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, presence penalty 1.5, and a 2,048-token response limit). The original TextSeal accuracy is from the team's earlier sampled evaluation with a different seed. The highest-entropy mask is selected over the full training corpus; dropped tokens remain in the context but contribute no SFT loss. Ablations: dropping a random 50% gives TextSeal $$1.5 \times 10^{-5}$$ and SynthID $$3.3 \times 10^{-4}$$; dropping the lowest-entropy 50% gives TextSeal $$5.2 \times 10^{-9}$$ and SynthID $$9.7 \times 10^{-7}$$. The clean control gives TextSeal 0.36 and SynthID 0.88.
