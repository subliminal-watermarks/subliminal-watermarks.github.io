<div class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**TL;DR**

We introduce two simple "laundering" techniques that defeat most existing anti-distillation watermarking and fingerprinting detectors. Motivated by the insight that watermark detection may be more robust in **parameter space**, we then introduce Subliminal-Watermark, a new detection scheme that identifies distillation from a single sampled rollout of a suspect model and remains effective after entropy laundering, even when its detector is trained on separate traces.
</div>

## 1. What do we solve?

Unauthorized distillation raises many legal, safety, and provenance concerns. The model provider needs a way to know
- "is this response generated by my model?"
- "is this response genereated by a model that distills from mine?"
- "does their model distill from mine?"

One scientific way to achieve this is to embed statistical patterns into the LLM's responses while disturbing the logit distribution as minimally as possible. This is known as _watermarking_. Quite often in the literature, the second question is referred to specifically as _antidistillation fingerprinting_. We will simply use the term _watermark_ from now on.

Notice that the second question subsumes the third one, if you have API access to the suspect model. For example, you could accuse a model for distillation if you query $$N$$ random responses from the suspect and they are all flagged as "watermarked." 
Of course, the higher $$N$$ is, the lower the false positive rate (FPR) and true positive rate (TPR) are.

We present a new detection scheme, Subliminal-Watermark. For our key-1 student, it flags all 800 held-out watermarked responses at a 2.75% false-positive rate on the clean control, using a detector trained on separate traces. Requiring all five answers to pass can sharply reduce false positives; [§5.5](#model-detection) quantifies this gain and its assumptions.

On the other hand, the distillers are smart too. They understand how LLM watermark works and might come up with effective attacks from first principles. So, we switch positions and play adversary now. We stress-test our detection scheme under two 'laundering' schemes:
- Grandchild Laundering
- Entropy Laundering

Both laundering schemes are capable of breaking all existing watermark/fingerprint techniques that we tried, including Gumbel-Max, SynthID, TextSeal, and ADFP. These are strong attacks!

Our detector remains effective after entropy laundering. Its separate-trace grandchild evaluation is still pending; we report only completed separate-trace detector results below.

## 2. Background {#background}

For watermarks, we followed Aaronson's Gumbel-Max watermark. The idea is simple: since there are intrinsic randomness in LLM sampling at non-zero temperature, we could swap in pseudorandomness that can be computed deterministically post-hoc. By the definition of pseudoramdomness, even though we distort the distribution at fixed prefix to embed preferences on some tokens, the preferences are random when we averge across prefixes.

More concretely, we prepare an $$n$$-gram hash table: at each step, the previous $$n$$ tokens and a secret key $$k$$ are hashed into a seed, and the seed generates one pseudorandom number $$u_v \in (0,1)$$ for every token $$v$$ in the vocabulary. Given the model's next-token probabilities $$p_v$$, return $$\arg\max_v \left[\log p_v + g_v\right]$$ with $$g_v = -\log(-\log u_v)$$, the standard Gumbel-Max trick. Because the seed only depends only on the preceeding $$n$$ tokens and the key, one can recompute every $$u_v$$ afterwards with the key.

The detection in the original work was to detect the _textual_ correlation between sampled tokens and the preferences computed from pseudoramdomness:

$$
S(y) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} -\ln\big(1 - u^{(t)}_{y_t}\big),
$$

where $$u^{(t)}_{y_t}$$ is the pseudorandom number at the $$t$$-th token, recomputed from the key and the preceeding $$n$$ tokens.

SynthID and TextSeal are based on the same Gumbel-Max idea and improve the quality-detectability tradeoff. Green/Red-list and ADFP is another line of technique. We won't elaborate further on them, because none of their detections survives through our laundering schemes, and once we switch to our new detection in parameter space, even the vanilla Gumbel-Max watermark becomes detectable after adversarial distillation.

## 4. Two laundering schemes {#laundering}

**Grandchild laundering.** Train a *child* on the watermarked teacher traces as usual, but don't deploy it. Instead, sample the child on the same prompts, keep the correct answers, and train a fresh *grandchild* from the original checkpoint on those. The grandchild never sees a watermarked token.

There are two issues here:
1. The train $$p$$-values for gradnchildren are  orders of magnitude higher than that of children.
2. Their detection does not even work for prompts besides the ones the distiller trains on. (To be clear, they all acknowledged this.)

**Entropy laundering.** Watermark embeds pattern in the pseudorandomness of choosing the next tokens. If the next token is fairly certain, then there is no way to change it without dramatically changing the output distribution. So, we simply drop the top 50% highest-entropy tokens (one threshold over the whole corpus) from the SFT loss, while keeping them in the context. Of course, since the distiller won't have teacher's logits, the entropy has to be approximated by the student's. But it is very effective to TextSeal, SynthID, and ADFP. ### 5.1 The detector

Let $$\theta_0$$ be the teacher. On its own prompts, the provider samples clean traces $$D_c$$ and watermarked traces $$D'_w$$, then fine-tunes two copies of the teacher with identical settings:

$$
\theta_c = \mathrm{SFT}(\theta_0, D_c), \qquad \theta_w = \mathrm{SFT}(\theta_0, D'_w).
$$

The two datasets differ only in the watermark, so $$\Delta\theta = \theta_w - \theta_c$$ is a watermark direction in weight space. For a suspect prompt-response pair $$(x, y)$$, the *likelihood score* is

$$
\begin{equation}
s(x, y) \;=\; \frac{1}{|y|} \Big\langle \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(y \mid x)\big|_{\theta_c},\; \Delta\theta \Big\rangle \;=\; \frac{1}{|y|}\, \frac{d}{d\epsilon} \log p_{\theta_c + \epsilon \Delta\theta}(y \mid x) \Big|_{\epsilon = 0} . \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
$$

In words: 

*How much more likely is the response if we shift the clean teacher toward the watermarked one in parameter space?*
{: .key-idea}

**Crucially**, the score can be computed very efficiently via autodiff. We just need one forward pass through the slightly perturbed weight $$\theta_c + \epsilon\Delta\theta$$ and then a backward pass to the scalar $$\epsilon$$. The gradients are _never materialized_. For those who like math, this is because our score is _not_ the cosine similarity between two gradients or two paraneter-space directions, but the canonical pairing between one of each (or between the tangent space and the cotangent space if you wish). This is why we don't need to, e.g., normalize the gradients and $$\Delta\theta$$ in princples, or approximate the Hessian of loss landscape.

In fact, even without backprop, the finite-difference approximation already works well:

$$
\begin{equation}
s(x, y) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{|y|} \log \frac{p_{\theta_w}(y \mid x)}{p_{\theta_c}(y \mid x)} , \label{eq:llr}
\end{equation}
$$

which is just the mean log-likelihood ratio between the teacher checkpoints. With two keys $$k_1, k_2$$, we fit one watermarked teacher per key and write $$s_1, s_2$$ for the corresponding scores.

### 5.5 Model detection: All $$N$$ Pass {#model-detection}

To audit a model, fix the key, score, and single-response threshold in advance. Query $$N$$ independently sampled prompts, and flag the model only if **all $$N$$ responses pass**. If a fixed clean model passes one query with probability $$q$$ and a fixed watermarked model passes with probability $$t$$, then

$$
\mathrm{FPR}_N = q^N, \qquad \mathrm{TPR}_N = t^N.
$$

For the key-1 **$$D'_w$$ alignment detector**, the held-out counts are 22/800 clean passes and 800/800 watermarked passes. They give the following estimates for fresh independent queries; superscripts and subscripts mark one-sided 95% confidence bounds:

| Queries $$N$$ | Estimated FPR | Estimated TPR |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $$2.75^{+1.16}\%$$ | $$100_{-0.37}\%$$ |
| 3 | $$2.08^{+3.87} \times 10^{-3}\%$$ | $$100_{-1.12}\%$$ |
| 5 | $$1.57^{+7.50} \times 10^{-6}\%$$ | $$100_{-1.85}\%$$ |

Five queries therefore look very effective **in this setup**. The tiny FPR is an extrapolation from held-out response counts, not a directly measured error rate across millions of models. Nor does 800/800 establish population-perfect TPR.

### 5.6 Coding-domain diagnostic {#ood-coding}

The science-trained students and $$D'_w$$ detector also distinguish watermarked from clean answers to LiveCodeBench v6 coding problems.

| Score | AUROC per answer | Key-1 answers flagged | Clean answers flagged | Key-1 problems flagged | Clean problems flagged |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alignment | $$0.9755 \pm 0.0083$$ | 300/396 (75.8%) | 8/396 (2.0%) | 99/99 | 6/99 |
| Log-likelihood ratio | $$0.9806 \pm 0.0081$$ | 296/396 (74.7%) | 8/396 (2.0%) | 99/99 | 7/99 |

*$$D'_w$$ · 99 held-out coding problems × four answers · coding-specific clean calibration on 42 other problems.*

For the problem-level columns, we average the scores of a problem's four answers and flag the problem if that average exceeds a cutoff set the same way as the per-answer cutoff, but from the averages of the 42 clean calibration problems. This is a different aggregation rule from “all $$N$$ pass.”

The per-answer numbers are less clean than on science, for two reasons. Much of it is threshold calibration: carrying the science cutoff over unchanged gives 84.1% TPR / 4.0% FPR for alignment and 99.2% TPR / 18.9% FPR for the likelihood ratio, so each domain needs its own cutoff. But single coding answers also overlap a little (per-answer AUROC 0.98), so no single-answer cutoff is perfect; averaging four answers per problem catches all 99 watermarked problems.


### 5.7 Interpretation

You might wonder "If it's just a likelihood test, do we really need to finetune the teacher? Can't we just use the Gumbel-Max distribution to compute the watermarked likelihood directly?" We tried a few versions of this, and they all worked on the teacher generated texts, but none survives through distillation. This seems to suggest that although the distributional difference between the clean and watermarked traces is generated by the Gumbel-Max pseudorandomness, that is _not_ what the models learns through SFT. Both the watermarked student and teacher seem to learn the same feature/circuit to explain the distributional difference that's different from the ground truth. We hope to report more details on this soon.

Our current hypothesis is that watermarks are more like a hint or trigger that incentivizes the model to represent a new feature/circuit. This new feature does not correctly invert the hash functions (thankfully!!😱), which is the ground truth, but is a universal one that models of different sizes would learn.

#### Training and sampling

- **Teacher and students.** The teacher is the same science-SFT Qwen3.5-9B adapter throughout. The detector-study students start from Qwen3.5-4B and use LoRA rank 32. Each clean or keyed student is SFT-trained on 9,000 teacher traces.
- **Detector rollouts.** Thinking mode; at most 1,024 new tokens per response. Sampling uses temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, and presence penalty 1.5.
- **Detector scoring.** All reported fitted detectors were trained on $$D'_w$$ and scored in FP32. The main science and entropy results retokenize returned response text without adding a synthetic EOS; explicit terminal special-token strings in the entropy responses remain part of that text. The coding diagnostic uses original generated token IDs, including any retained terminal EOS, and is labeled separately. Scoring keeps the full prompt and the first response tokens up to a 1,024-token total limit; only response tokens enter the per-token score.
- **Watermarks.** Our detector experiments use vanilla Gumbel-Max (TextSeal with $$\alpha = 0$$), a 3-token hash context, and keys $$k_1, k_2$$. The laundering comparisons use TextSeal with $$\alpha = 0.1$$, SynthID, and ADFP. ADFP uses $$\lambda = 256$$ in the grandchild table and $$\lambda = 64$$ in the entropy table.

#### Prompt partitions and detector labels

- $$D'_w$$ is the fresh detector-training corpus; $$D_w$$ denotes the student's training corpus, not a second set of detector results. This post excludes detector results fitted on $$D_w$$.
- **Decontamination.** The recorded near-duplicate audit found no matches between fresh detector-training, student-training, and detector-evaluation prompts. Our additional exact-prompt check found zero overlap between the 9,000 fresh detector-training prompts and either of the two 1,000-prompt science evaluation pools. The two evaluation pools also have no exact-prompt overlap.
- **Detector evaluation.** The main two-key and entropy studies use different 1,000-prompt science pools. Each has 200 calibration, 400 development, and 400 confirmation prompts. We preserve each study's own split and combine development and confirmation into 800 evaluation prompts for display; no pooled evaluation outcome is used to fit a cutoff.
- **Threshold.** For each detector score, sort the 200 clean calibration scores and use the 191st-smallest as the cutoff. Flag a response only if its score is **strictly greater** than that cutoff. The 800 plotted outcomes do not set the threshold. This nominal calibration rule does not guarantee an empirical 5% FPR on every evaluation set.
- **Key contrast.** The zero line for $$s_1 - s_2$$ compares the two key scores. It is not the threshold for deciding whether a response is watermarked.
- **Uncertainty and scope.** These are exploratory reanalyses of existing responses, with one fitted student per condition. Science response-rate intervals use exact Clopper–Pearson bounds; AUROC intervals use 2,000 paired-prompt bootstrap resamples. A bootstrap interval of [1, 1] for perfect ranking is not a guarantee of zero population error. Response resampling does not replicate model training.

#### All-N model audits

For $$h$$ passing responses among $$M$$ saved evaluation prompts, choosing $$N$$ distinct prompts uniformly without replacement gives the exact finite-pool pass probability

$$
P_{\mathrm{pool}}(\text{all pass}) = \frac{\binom{h}{N}}{\binom{M}{N}}.
$$

For key-1 alignment this is $$\binom{22}{5}/\binom{800}{5} = 9.77 \times 10^{-9}$$ for the clean pool, and 1 for the watermarked pool. These describe the saved pools, not unseen models. The fresh-iid table in §5.5 instead uses $$(h/M)^N$$; its one-sided confidence limits are the corresponding one-sided 95% Clopper–Pearson limits raised to $$N$$. Each bound is marginal for the stated fitted student and fixed cutoff, not simultaneous across every method and cohort.

| Cohort ($$D'_w$$) | Score | Five-query TPR estimate | Five-query FPR estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key 1, original | Alignment | 100% | $$1.57 \times 10^{-6}\%$$ |
| Key 1, original | Likelihood ratio | 100% | $$6.29 \times 10^{-5}\%$$ |
| Key 2, original | Alignment | 90.97% | $$1.05 \times 10^{-4}\%$$ |
| Key 2, original | Likelihood ratio | 99.38% | $$1.54 \times 10^{-4}\%$$ |
| Key 1, entropy-laundered | Alignment | 65.91% | $$7.59 \times 10^{-8}\%$$ |
| Key 1, entropy-laundered | Likelihood ratio | 99.38% | $$3.99 \times 10^{-5}\%$$ |

The null assumption matters. Calibration answers and new clean audit answers must come from the same score distribution. For a clean null matching the calibration model, the 191st order statistic among 200 continuous iid scores gives a calibration-random exceedance probability $$q\sim\mathrm{Beta}(10,191)$$. Averaged over calibration samples,

$$
\Pr(\text{all }N\text{ clean answers pass})
= \mathbb E[q^N]
= \prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{10+j}{201+j}.
$$

At $$N=5$$ this is $$6.97 \times 10^{-7}$$ as a probability, or **$$6.97 \times 10^{-5}\%$$**. This calibration-marginal result is distinct from a conditional guarantee for every realized threshold; strict exceedance is conservative with ties. Correlated or adaptively chosen prompts, repeated answers to one problem, and a different clean model do not justify simply raising the original clean rate to the fifth power.

For the additional entropy-trained clean model, transferring the original science cutoff gives 14/800 alignment passes and 53/800 likelihood-ratio passes. Their five-query iid FPR estimates are $$1.64 \times 10^{-7}\%$$ and $$1.28 \times 10^{-4}\%$$, respectively. The original and entropy-trained clean checkpoints have distinct recorded weight hashes. This is one additional clean-model check, not coverage of arbitrary clean checkpoints.

- **Coding cutoffs.** The per-answer rates in §5.6 are recomputed with the same order-statistic convention as the science study: rank $$\lceil0.95(m+1)\rceil$$ among the $$m=168$$ retained clean calibration answers, hence rank 161. We use problem-clustered uncertainty and separately report the unchanged science-cutoff transfer. These explicit cutoffs can differ from preliminary Slack percentages.
- **Complete results.** Both keyed detectors are complete on the three main science students. Key-1 alignment and likelihood ratios are complete for both entropy arms and for coding clean versus key 1.
- **Pending results.** The separate-trace grandchild and additional unlaundered replication on the entropy study's prompt pool lack complete merged score files after SIGTERM failures. Coding key-1 responses under detector 2 and key-2 responses under both detectors are also incomplete. Partial files are preserved and are not reported as completed results. No complete $$D'_w$$ dilution result was found, so no dilution result or historical threshold is included.

#### Laundering evaluations

- **Grandchild table.** For SynthID and TextSeal, "train" uses 3,000 traces the child trained on; "held-out" uses traces on prompts no student saw. Accuracy (%) is measured on 1,000 held-out science MCQs. We have not verified whether these MCQ prompt IDs coincide with the detector-study prompt IDs.
- **ADFP.** The displayed grandchild $$p$$-values use the teacher's training traces. At teacher-trace prefixes, the evaluator samples the student's next token and measures its keyed green-list membership, keeping the first occurrence of each evaluated context bigram. The reported $$p$$-value is the one-sided Hoeffding bound: $$p = \exp[-2N(\bar g - \gamma)^2]$$ when $$\bar g > \gamma$$, and $$p = 1$$ otherwise, with green-list fraction $$\gamma = 0.5$$. Here $$N$$ is the number of retained measurements and $$\bar g$$ their mean. It is a corpus-level statistic, not a per-response FPR. The clean training $$p$$-value and accuracy were not reported for this ADFP grandchild comparison.
- **Entropy table.** TextSeal and SynthID cells report $$p$$-value / entropy-weighted $$p$$-value (accuracy); ADFP cells report $$p$$-value (accuracy). Detection is evaluated on training traces. The highest-entropy mask is selected over the full training corpus; dropped tokens remain in the context but contribute no SFT loss.
