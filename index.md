<div class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**TL;DR**

Are the current watermarking schemes robust against adversarial distillation? We first introduce two simple techniques that defeat all anti-distillation watermark detectors we tested: **grandchild** and **entropy laundering**. Inspired by subliminal learning, we suspect that the watermark signal may generalize better in **parameter space**, and introduce **Subliminal-Watermark**, a new detection scheme that can identify distillation from a few sampled rollouts of a suspect model and remains effective after the laundering schemes. It asks "how much does the likelihood of this sequence increase if we shift our probe model's weight toward a watermarked one?". 

On science questions, Subliminal-Watermark achieves about **99%** true positive rate (TPR) and **3%** false positive rate (FPR) per **single** query to the suspect. This also means that a simple majority vote over, say, 10 sampled rollouts, is projected to detect distillation almost perfectly. On coding tasks, the detection is more difficult, likely because code tokens have lower entropy, but Subliminal-Watermark still achieves above **65%** TPR at **5%** FPR. A majority vote over only 50 sampled rollouts again is projected to achieve almost perfect detection.

## 1. What is an LLM watermark? {#watermark}

When a frontier model is served through an API, anyone can collect the responses and train their own model on them. This is known as _distillation_. Without permision, this raises legal, safety, and provenance concerns. The model provider needs a way to know
- "is this response generated by my model?"
- "is this response genereated by a model that distills from mine?"
- "does their model distill from mine?"

The standard tool is _watermarking_ ([Aaronson, 2022](#ref-aaronson2022)): embed statistical patterns into the LLM's responses while disturbing the logit distribution as minimally as possible. Sometimes the second question is called _antidistillation fingerprinting_ ([Xu et al., 2026](#ref-xu2026)), but we will simply use the term _watermark_. Of course, if we can answer the second question, then we also solve the third one if we can query the model, e.g., majority vote on the queried responses from the suspect.

The best example is Aaronson's ([2022](#ref-aaronson2022)) Gumbel-Max watermark. The idea is simple: since there are intrinsic randomness in LLM sampling at non-zero temperature anyways, we could swap in pseudorandomness that can be deterministically verified later.

More concretely, we prepare an $$n$$-gram hash table: at each step, the previous $$n$$ tokens and a secret key $$k$$ are hashed into a seed, and the seed generates one pseudorandom number $$u_v \in (0,1)$$ for every token $$v$$ in the vocabulary. Given the model's next-token probabilities $$p_v$$, return $$\arg\max_v u_v^{1/p_v}$$, the standard Gumbel-Max trick. Because the seed only depends only on the preceeding $$n$$ tokens and the key, one can recompute every $$u_v$$ afterwards with the key. 

If you are not familiar with this, don't worry. Click on the random key below, and see how it changes the model's output. You can also click on the blue tokens to see $$p_v$$ and $$u_v$$ explicitly.

To anyone without the key, $$u$$ looks uniformly random, so each token is still chosen with probability $$p_v$$.


The detection in the original work compute the _textual_ correlation between sampled tokens $$y_t$$ and the pseudoramdomness numbers $$u_{y_t}$$ by $$ S(y) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} -\ln\big(1 - u^{(t)}_{y_t}\big).$$ Again, the number $$u_{y_t}$$ can be recomputed from the key $$k$$ and the preceeding $$n$$ tokens. 

Follow-ups, such as SynthID and TextSeal, all work similarly but improve the quality-detectability tradeoff. Green/Red-list ([Kirchenbauer et al., 2023](#ref-kirchenbauer2023)) and ADFP work slightly differently, but we won't elaborate further. This is because none of these detections survives through the simple attacks introduced in the next section, and once we switch to our new detection method, Subliminal-Watermark, even the vanilla Gumbel-Max watermark becomes detectable after the attacks.

## 2. Two simple attacks defeat previous detection methods {#laundering}

Are the current watermarking schemes robust against smart distillers? Unfortunately, the answer is _no_. In fact, they don't even need sophisticated tricks, but just understand how LLM watermark works from first principles. We introduce two simple attacks

**Grandchild laundering.** Train a *child* on the watermarked teacher traces as usual, but don't deploy it. Instead, sample the child on the same prompts, keep the correct answers, and train a fresh *grandchild* from the original checkpoint on those. The grandchild never sees a watermarked token.

## 3. Subliminal-Watermark: a novel, robust detection in parameter space {#robust-detection}


### 3.1 The detector

Let $$\theta_0$$ be the teacher. On its own prompts, the provider samples clean traces $$D_c$$ and watermarked traces $$D'_w$$, then fine-tunes two copies of the teacher with identical settings:

$$
\theta_c = \mathrm{SFT}(\theta_0, D_c), \qquad \theta_w = \mathrm{SFT}(\theta_0, D'_w).
$$

The two datasets differ only in the watermark, so $$\Delta\theta = \theta_w - \theta_c$$ is a watermark direction in weight space. For a suspect prompt-response pair $$(x, y)$$, the *likelihood score* is

$$
\begin{equation}
s(x, y) \;=\; \frac{1}{|y|} \Big\langle \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(y \mid x)\big|_{\theta_c},\; \Delta\theta \Big\rangle \;=\; \frac{1}{|y|}\, \frac{d}{d\epsilon} \log p_{\theta_c + \epsilon \Delta\theta}(y \mid x) \Big|_{\epsilon = 0} . \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
$$

In words: 

*How much more likely is the response if we shift the clean teacher toward the watermarked one in parameter space?*
{: .key-idea}

<div class="anim-embed">
<iframe src="{{ '/assets/anim/detector.html' | relative_url }}" title="Animation: how the parameter-space detector works" loading="lazy"></iframe>
</div>

**Crucially**, the score can be computed very efficiently via autodiff. We just need one forward pass through the slightly perturbed weight $$\theta_c + \epsilon\Delta\theta$$ and then a backward pass to the scalar $$\epsilon$$. The gradients are _never materialized_. For those who like math, this is because our score is _not_ the cosine similarity between two gradients or two paraneter-space directions, but the canonical pairing between one of each (or between the tangent space and the cotangent space if you wish). This is why we don't need to, e.g., normalize the gradients and $$\Delta\theta$$ in princples, or approximate the Hessian of loss landscape.

In fact, even without backprop, the finite-difference approximation already works well:

$$
\begin{equation}
s(x, y) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{|y|} \log \frac{p_{\theta_w}(y \mid x)}{p_{\theta_c}(y \mid x)} , \label{eq:llr}
\end{equation}
$$

which is just the **mean log-likelihood ratio** (LLR) between the teacher checkpoints. Despite its strong performance in practice, this simple score is less interpretable from linear parameter-space directions.

What if both OpenAI and Anthropic decide to use our watermarking scheme? Then we can not only detect distillation, but the _source_ of distillation! With detectors for both key 1 and key 2 trained on $$D'_w$$, the contrast $$s_1 - s_2$$ separates the key-1 and key-2 students perfectly (AUROC 1.000 on 800 held-out prompts). All 800 key-1 responses have positive contrast, and all 800 key-2 responses have negative contrast.

### 3.5 Model detection: majority vote {#model-detection}

To audit a model, fix the key, score, and clean-teacher-calibrated single-response threshold in advance. Query $$N$$ independently sampled prompts and flag the model if **a strict majority of responses pass**. For $$N=10$$, at least six responses must pass; a five–five tie counts as negative.

If a fixed clean model passes one query with probability $$q$$ and a fixed watermarked model passes with probability $$t$$, the independent-query projections are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{FPR}_N &= \Pr\!\left[\operatorname{Binomial}(N,q) \ge \lfloor N/2\rfloor+1\right],\\
\mathrm{TPR}_N &= \Pr\!\left[\operatorname{Binomial}(N,t) \ge \lfloor N/2\rfloor+1\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the teacher-calibrated science response rates from the 800 held-out prompts gives:

| Study | Key | Score | Projected TPR, $$N=10$$ | Projected FPR, $$N=10$$ (probability) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 1 | <span class="score-label" markdown="span">Alignment $$\eqref{eq:score}$$</span> | 100%* | $$4.70 \times 10^{-8}$$ |
| Science | 1 | <span class="score-label" markdown="span">LLR $$\eqref{eq:llr}$$</span> | 100%* | $$8.26 \times 10^{-8}$$ |
| Science | 2 | <span class="score-label" markdown="span">Alignment $$\eqref{eq:score}$$</span> | 99.98883% | $$1.25 \times 10^{-8}$$ |
| Science | 2 | <span class="score-label" markdown="span">LLR $$\eqref{eq:llr}$$</span> | 99.99999977% | $$1.38 \times 10^{-7}$$ |
| Entropy laundering | 1 | <span class="score-label" markdown="span">Alignment $$\eqref{eq:score}$$</span> | 99.89243% | $$1.35 \times 10^{-9}$$ |

The tiny FPRs are not directly measured error rates across a population of clean models. Correlated queries or repeated answers to one problem need separate validation, and teacher calibration does not guarantee the same FPR for an unfamiliar clean model.

### 3.7 Interpretation

You might wonder "If it's just a likelihood test, do we really need to finetune the teacher? Can't we just use the Gumbel-Max distribution to compute the watermarked likelihood directly?" We tried a few versions of this, and they all worked well on the teacher-generated texts, but none survives through distillation. This seems to suggest that although the distributional difference between the clean and watermarked traces theoreticall comes from the Gumbel-Max pseudorandomness, that is _not_ what the models learns through SFT. Both the watermarked student and teacher seem to learn the same feature/circuit to explain the distributional difference that's different from the true watermark distribution. We hope to report more details on this soon.

Our current hypothesis is that watermarks are more like a trigger that incentivizes the model to learn some rare feature that could not be learned during usual training. This new feature does not correctly invert the hash functions (thankfully!!😱), which is the ground truth, but is a universal one that models of different sizes (at least both 4B student and 9B teacher in our case) would learn.

#### Training and sampling

- **Teacher and students.** The science experiments use the same science-SFT Qwen3.5-9B adapter. Their students start from Qwen3.5-4B and use LoRA rank 32. Each first-generation clean or keyed student is SFT-trained on 9,000 teacher traces; grandchildren train on child-generated traces.
- **Science detector rollouts.** Thinking mode; at most 1,024 new tokens per response. Sampling uses temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, and presence penalty 1.5.
- **Science detector scoring.** The science-fitted $$D'_w$$ detectors are scored in FP32. The main science, entropy, grandchild, and clean-teacher calibration results retokenize returned response text without adding a synthetic EOS; explicit terminal special-token strings remain part of that text. Scoring keeps the full prompt and the first response tokens up to a 1,024-token total limit; only response tokens enter the per-token score.
- **Watermarks.** The science detector experiments use vanilla Gumbel-Max (TextSeal with $$\alpha = 0$$), a 3-token hash context, and keys $$k_1, k_2$$. The baseline laundering comparisons in §2 use TextSeal with $$\alpha = 0.1$$, SynthID, and ADFP. ADFP uses $$\lambda = 256$$ in the grandchild table and $$\lambda = 64$$ in the entropy table.

#### Science prompt partitions and detector labels

- $$D'_w$$ is the fresh detector-training corpus; $$D_w$$ denotes the student's training corpus, not a second set of detector results. This post excludes detector results fitted on $$D_w$$.
- **Decontamination.** The recorded near-duplicate audit found no matches between fresh detector-training, student-training, and detector-evaluation prompts. Our additional exact-prompt check found zero overlap between the 9,000 fresh detector-training prompts and either of the two 1,000-prompt science evaluation pools. The two evaluation pools also have no exact-prompt overlap.
- **Detector evaluation.** The main two-key study uses one 1,000-prompt science pool; entropy and grandchild laundering share another. We retain their original 400 development and 400 confirmation prompts and combine them into 800 evaluation prompts per study. The remaining 200 prompts were used in earlier student-calibrated analyses; they do not set the clean-teacher cutoffs reported here.
- **Clean-teacher calibration.** We generate 1,000 unwatermarked responses from the source teacher $$\theta_0$$ with the same thinking-mode sampling settings as the student evaluations. These prompts are disjoint from student training, detector training, all earlier science evaluation pools, and the additional student-calibration prompt pool. Both keys score this same teacher corpus. The clean source teacher $$\theta_0$$ is distinct from the fitted clean detector reference $$\theta_c$$.
- **Threshold.** For each key and score, sort the 1,000 clean source-teacher scores and use the 951st-smallest as the cutoff. Flag a response only if its score is **strictly greater** than that cutoff. Freeze the teacher cutoffs before computing the student-transfer results; clean-student responses do not fit these thresholds. The same key-1 cutoffs apply unchanged to the main, entropy, and grandchild students. The nominal 5% teacher-calibration target does not guarantee a 5% FPR for a different clean model.
- **Key contrast.** The zero line for $$s_1 - s_2$$ compares the two key scores. It is not the threshold for deciding whether a response is watermarked.
- **Uncertainty and scope.** Teacher cutoffs are frozen before the transfer analysis, but the student evaluation responses had been inspected historically, so these are exploratory transfer results with one fitted student per condition. Science response-rate intervals use exact Clopper–Pearson bounds; AUROC intervals use paired-prompt bootstrap resamples. A bootstrap interval of [1, 1] for perfect ranking is not a guarantee of zero population error. #### Coding detection

The coding experiment in §3.6 uses its own SWESmith-SFT Qwen3-4B source teacher and separately fitted coding references. Keyed coding traces use TextSeal with $$\alpha = 0$$ and a 3-token hash context. Generation allows up to 28,672 prompt tokens and 4,096 response tokens within a 32,768-token context. Coding LLR scores the original generated response token IDs, including a terminal EOS when emitted, conditioned on the full prompt.

Calibration generates unwatermarked actions from that source teacher at temperature 1, top-k 20, top-p 0.95, with a 4,096-token response budget; watermark injection is disabled and outputs are not filtered by success. Each detector uses the 951st-smallest score from the 1,000-action clean-teacher pool, with a strict greater-than decision. This gives a shared child/grandchild cutoff of **0.014877768768661323** for per-token LLR and **1.5491128392910267** for the conventional TextSeal score $$-\log_{10}p$$. Confirmation models and scores are held fixed while replacing the earlier clean-student calibration. Coding and science use their own fitted references and thresholds.

#### Reported scope

Both keyed science detectors are complete on the three main science students. Key-1 results are complete for entropy and grandchild laundering. Coding reports the child and grandchild lineage in §3.6. Additional replication and dilution analyses are outside the scope of this post.

#### Baseline laundering evaluations (§2)

- **Grandchild table.** For SynthID and TextSeal, the $$p$$-values use 3,000 traces the child trained on. The clean control gives SynthID 0.35 and TextSeal 0.82.
- **ADFP.** The displayed grandchild $$p$$-values use the teacher's training traces. At teacher-trace prefixes, the evaluator samples the student's next token and measures its keyed green-list membership, keeping the first occurrence of each evaluated context bigram. The reported $$p$$-value is the one-sided Hoeffding bound: $$p = \exp[-2N(\bar g - \gamma)^2]$$ when $$\bar g > \gamma$$, and $$p = 1$$ otherwise, with green-list fraction $$\gamma = 0.5$$. Here $$N$$ is the number of retained measurements and $$\bar g$$ their mean. It is a corpus-level statistic, not a per-response FPR. The clean training $$p$$-value was not reported for this ADFP grandchild comparison.
- **Entropy table.** Cells report the usual $$p$$-value. Detection is evaluated on training traces. The highest-entropy mask is selected over the full training corpus; dropped tokens remain in the context but contribute no SFT loss. Ablations: dropping a random 50% gives TextSeal $$1.5 \times 10^{-5}$$ and SynthID $$3.3 \times 10^{-4}$$; dropping the lowest-entropy 50% gives TextSeal $$5.2 \times 10^{-9}$$ and SynthID $$9.7 \times 10^{-7}$$. The clean control gives TextSeal 0.36 and SynthID 0.88.
